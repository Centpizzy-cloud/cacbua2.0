China's Zero-Tariff Policy for Africa: What the 2026 Expansion Means for African Exporters

A practical policy brief examining new market-access opportunities, the limits of tariff removal, and what African businesses should prepare for when approaching the Chinese market.

Publication 001 August 2026 Review copy — not approved for publication

China's expansion of zero-tariff treatment to African countries in 2026 represents an important development in China–Africa commercial relations. For African exporters, however, tariff-free access alone does not guarantee successful entry into the Chinese market.

The greater opportunity lies in combining improved tariff treatment with export readiness: understanding Chinese import requirements, identifying products with realistic demand, meeting sanitary and technical standards, establishing reliable logistics, and developing relationships with Chinese buyers and distribution partners.

For African businesses and institutions, the policy therefore creates both an opportunity and a challenge. The opportunity is improved access to one of the world's largest consumer and industrial markets. The challenge is ensuring African producers have the capacity, information, certification, financing, logistics, and commercial connections necessary to use that access effectively.

This policy brief examines what changed, where opportunities may exist, what barriers remain, and what African businesses should consider before entering the Chinese market.

1. What changed in 2026?

On 14 February 2026, Chinese President Xi Jinping announced that China would grant zero-tariff treatment to all African countries holding diplomatic relations with China. This built on a policy already in place since 1 December 2024, which had eliminated tariffs on all tariff lines for Africa's 33 least-developed countries under the same diplomatic-recognition condition.

The 2026 expansion extended zero-tariff treatment to a further 20 African countries not classified as least-developed. It took effect on 1 May 2026 and, per China's State Council, runs through 30 April 2028 for those 20 countries.

Combined, the policy now covers 53 of the 54 African countries that hold diplomatic relations with China. Eswatini is the sole exclusion, as it maintains diplomatic ties with Taiwan rather than China.

Government sources describe the change as applying to "all tariff lines" — meaning the removal is not limited to a specific product list. That breadth is precisely why Section 3, below, matters: a zero tariff rate does not by itself mean a product can freely enter the Chinese market.

2. Where could African businesses benefit?

01

Agriculture & processed food

Products such as dried chilies, coffee, and cashews gained unified duty-free access under the policy.

02

High-value agricultural exports

Nigeria's aquatic products, for example, separately secured zero-tariff access under a new bilateral protocol.

03

Manufacturing

Producers assembling or processing goods in Africa may find new price competitiveness, though scale and certification requirements vary widely by sector.

04

Minerals & value-added processing

Tariff-free access may improve the economics of processing minerals in Africa rather than exporting them raw — subject to separate investment and infrastructure conditions.

05

Renewable-energy supply chains

Components and materials feeding renewable-energy manufacturing may see improved market access, though this is a developing area, not an established trend.

06

Selected consumer products

Nigerian goods including sesame, cattle bone granules, and liquefied propane are named by Chinese authorities as specific early beneficiaries.

These are illustrative categories, not guarantees. Not every product in a category automatically qualifies, and tariff-free treatment does not by itself guarantee commercial success — see Section 3.

3. Zero tariff does not mean zero barriers

A zero tariff rate removes one cost, not every obstacle. Exporters approaching the Chinese market still face the same practical requirements they would face regardless of the tariff rate:

4. Nigeria–China opportunity

Nigeria offers a concrete early picture of the policy's effect. According to Chinese Ambassador to Nigeria Yu Dunhai, Nigeria–China trade rose 35% to $18 billion in the first half of 2026 following the zero-tariff rollout. Chinese imports from Nigeria specifically surged 80% to $2.3 billion over the same period, with monthly growth in Nigerian exports to China exceeding 40% in both May and June — the first two months the policy was in effect.

Chinese authorities have named sesame, cattle bone granules, and liquefied propane as specific Nigerian products benefiting from the tariff concessions, and Nigeria's aquatic products separately secured zero-tariff access under a new bilateral protocol.

These figures describe national trade data, not CACBUA-facilitated trade — CACBUA has not organized or claimed credit for any of these transactions, and this brief does not imply otherwise.

5. From market access to market readiness

The following is CACBUA's own practical checklist for a business evaluating the Chinese market under this policy — it is analysis, not a sourced statistic.

01

Identify the export product.

02

Confirm whether it is eligible to enter China.

03

Determine applicable Chinese standards and certification.

04

Research actual Chinese market demand.

05

Calculate production, logistics, customs, and landed costs.

06

Identify legitimate buyers and distributors.

07

Prepare suitable commercial and product documentation.

08

Consider Chinese-language materials where appropriate.

09

Verify payment and contractual arrangements.

10

Obtain appropriate customs, legal, or professional advice before committing to a transaction.

6. CACBUA perspective

From policy access to commercial access

The significance of China's zero-tariff initiative should ultimately be measured not simply by the number of tariff lines reduced, but by whether African enterprises can increase competitive, value-added exports to the Chinese market.

African institutions, trade organizations, and businesses can use this period to strengthen export readiness, market intelligence, and direct commercial relationships. CACBUA can contribute by supporting access to reliable information and creating channels for dialogue, understanding, and cooperation between institutions and businesses across China and Africa.

This section presents a proposed CACBUA institutional perspective for review. It does not claim that CACBUA facilitated any specific trade, investment, or government policy.
